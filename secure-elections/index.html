NSRA Title XL · Sec. 4006 · National Verifiable Voting System

Elections any citizen can check, from your own phone

Today you are asked to trust the count. Under the NSRA you can check it. Every ballot, from Congress down to school board, is posted in encrypted form to a public ledger as it is counted. You confirm your own ballot was counted with a code. Anyone can verify every total with free software. And no one, not an official, a party, a vendor or a hacker, can see how you voted.

Why it matters

More than 158 million ballots were cast in 2024 (Election Assistance Commission). A year later, only 60% of Americans were confident that votes in the 2026 midterms would be counted accurately nationwide, and trust had fallen among Democrats, Republicans and independents alike (UC San Diego Yankelovich Center, December 2025 to January 2026). Whoever loses, a large share of the country doubts the result. The fix is not asking people to trust harder. It is letting them check.

How it works

StepWhat happens
1. You vote on paperMark your ballot by hand or on an accessible device. The paper ballot is the official record.
2. It is scanned and encryptedThe scanner locks your choices with encryption and gives you a confirmation code. Mail ballots get the same treatment when they arrive.
3. It goes on the public ledgerEvery accepted ballot, still encrypted, is posted to a public ledger anyone can watch from any device, as the count happens.
4. You check your voteEnter your code on your phone and see that your ballot is on the ledger and was counted, or why it was not accepted.
5. Anyone checks the totalsTotals are computed from the encrypted ballots, posted at least hourly with a mathematical proof that anyone can verify with free software.
6. Paper confirms itBefore certification, states audit the paper ballots against the ledger. If they ever disagree, a hand count of the paper decides.

Why it is secure

ProtectionWhat the bill requires
Offline votingNo machine that records or counts votes is connected to the internet during voting or counting (Sec. 4006(f)(4)).
Split keysThe key that unlocks the totals is split among at least five trustees, including one from each major party and an independent. No one can unlock it alone. Individual ballots are never unlocked (Sec. 4006(h)).
Secret ballotYour code proves your ballot was counted, never who you voted for, so no one can buy or force your vote (Sec. 4006(i)).
Open source, publicly ownedThe public owns the code, anyone can inspect it, and any manufacturer can build equipment for it, so no county depends on one vendor (Sec. 4006(c)).
Attacked before it is trustedIndependent hackers test it and a public bug bounty runs before every federal election, and no software changes are allowed in the last 90 days except certified security fixes (Sec. 4006(k)).
Copies everywhereThe ledger is held by the state, the federal commission and at least two independent archives, so no one can quietly change it (Sec. 4006(g)(5)).

Every ballot, local to national

One ballot usually carries races for Congress, governor, county, city council, school board and ballot measures. The system handles all of them on the same ballot, including multi-seat races, ranked-choice races where a state uses them, write-ins, every language required by the Voting Rights Act, and private, independent voting for people with disabilities. It is required for congressional elections. States that use it for every race on the ballot, local included, get federal grants that pay for the equipment.

Already proven in real elections

This kind of system, called end-to-end verifiable voting, is not theory. Voters in Fulton, Wisconsin used it in 2020, and voters in Franklin County, Idaho used it in the 2022 midterms. The federal voting-system standards adopted in 2021 already allow it. The NSRA builds it once, for the whole country, and gives it to every state for free.

What about voting from your phone?

You can check your vote from your phone on day one. Casting it from your phone comes later, and only when it is safe. Today the Department of Homeland Security, the FBI, the Election Assistance Commission and NIST rate sending ballots over the internet as high-risk. So the bill starts pilots within three years for military and overseas voters and voters with disabilities, and opens phone voting more widely only after federal security agencies certify it and Congress votes to approve it (Sec. 4006(m)).

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I see how I voted on the public ledger?

No. The ledger shows that your encrypted ballot was counted, never your choices. That protects you from anyone who would pressure or pay you to vote a certain way.

Is this internet voting?

No. You still vote on paper, and the machines that count are kept offline. The internet is used only to publish the encrypted ledger and the results so anyone can check them. Voting from a personal device comes only after pilots, federal security certification and a vote of Congress.

Does it work for local elections?

Yes. It handles every race on the ballot, from Congress to school board and ballot measures. It is required for congressional races, and states that use it for every race get grants that pay for the equipment.

What if the computers are hacked?

Three layers catch it. Anyone can check the math on the published totals, voters can confirm their own ballots, and the paper ballots are audited against the ledger before results are certified. If anything disagrees, a hand count of the paper decides.

Who controls it?

States and counties still run their elections. The federal role is the open-source software, standards, security testing and funding. The keys to the totals are split among trustees from both major parties and an independent, and a technical board with equal numbers from each party advises the program.

Does it change who can vote?

No. It does not change voter eligibility or registration rules, which stay with the states. It changes how ballots are counted and how anyone can check the count.

Has this been done before?

Yes. End-to-end verifiable voting was used in public elections in Fulton, Wisconsin in 2020 and Franklin County, Idaho in 2022, and federal voting-system standards adopted in 2021 already allow it.
