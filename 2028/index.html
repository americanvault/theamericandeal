A Ready-to-Introduce Cost-of-Living Agenda for 2028

The National Sovereignty and Resilience Act (NSRA) is a complete, section-numbered federal discussion draft built around one question voters keep asking: why does everything cost so much? It gives a 2028 campaign or a new member of Congress statutory text, a section-by-section, and a fiscal model to start from. It is not a slogan.

Why this draft, why now

  • Health costs are rising again. The enhanced ACA premium tax credits expired at the end of 2025, and the uninsured count is climbing in 2026. Title XXI sets up a zero-premium Basic Medical Provision delivered through competing private carriers.
  • Housing is a bipartisan issue. On January 20, 2026, the President directed agencies to curb purchases of single-family homes by large institutional investors and asked Congress to put that into law. Title XIV does: a 50-home-per-metro cap with a market-protected divestiture.
  • The stock-trading debate is unfinished. The House-passed 2026 bill still lets members keep individual stocks. Title XXIX is a full ban covering Congress, senior executive officials, and federal judges.
  • America has stopped building at speed. China built the world’s largest high-speed rail and ultra-high-voltage grid on standardized designs; Japan inspects every bridge and tunnel every five years. Title XVII brings that discipline home: hydropower at existing dams, mandatory maintenance, published unit costs, and permits on a fixed clock.
  • Drug prices remain the top health complaint. Title XIII ties U.S. prices to the G7 median and caps insulin at $35 a month.

How a campaign or office can use it

  • Adopt the whole framework as a signature legislative platform, or pull single titles into stand-alone bills.
  • Rename and re-sponsor freely. Attribution terms are set out in the Adoption & Attribution Guidance.
  • Brief staff quickly with the Executive Brief, Section-by-Section, and Title Briefs.
  • Stress-test the numbers in the interactive fiscal model and the Fiscal Scorebook. Known weak points are listed openly in Open Technical Questions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Has the NSRA been introduced in Congress?

No. The National Sovereignty and Resilience Act is a discussion draft. It has not been introduced in, considered by, or passed by either chamber. It is published so that candidates, members, and committee staff can review it before introduction in the 121st Congress or beyond.

Can a candidate or member adopt only part of the bill?

Yes. The draft is organized into 36 numbered titles, and most can be introduced as stand-alone bills. Any sponsor may adopt, rename, split, or amend it, with attribution as described in the Adoption & Attribution Guidance.

Is the NSRA tied to a political party?

No. It is citizen-drafted and not affiliated with any party, PAC, campaign, or elected official. Its core provisions, such as a congressional stock trading ban, an institutional homebuyer cap, and lower drug prices, already draw support from members of both parties.

Has the NSRA been scored by CBO?

Not yet. The fiscal figures are internal models built on published CBO and federal baselines and are labeled as such. Formal CBO and JCT scoring would follow introduction.
