Hard questions, answered straight

Every big bill has costs as well as benefits. These are the questions critics ask about the NSRA, with the answers our own numbers give, including the ones that cut against us. All of it can be checked: the model inputs and results are published, and CBO has not yet scored the bill.

What our own economic model shows

We ran the open-source OG-USA model of the U.S. economy on the bill as written, including the middle-class tax cut. We ran it ourselves, so it is our analysis, not an independent score.

The trade is plain: more money in the pockets of most families now, coverage for about 25 million people, and federal debt still falling, in exchange for a somewhat smaller economy and less business investment for the first two decades. An earlier version of the bill, without the middle-class tax cut, cut the debt twice as fast and ended with more investment, but left the middle of the income range slightly behind. We chose the middle class. You can judge for yourself; both runs are published. This run reflects the November 9 version of the bill with all 41 titles, including Medicare at 50, the limit on hospital prices, the science title and public compute (counted as public investment), and the global health fund (counted as spending). Medicare at 50 and the hospital price limit put more money in families’ hands and lead some people to work a little less; research and public compute add a little to long-run output.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will the NSRA shrink the economy?

In our model, modestly: GDP is about 1.9% lower in Year 1, 1.7% lower in Year 10 and 1.4% lower in Year 20, while federal debt falls by about 11 points of GDP by Year 20. The middle-class tax cut is the main reason the economy and investment recover more slowly than in our earlier version; it puts more money in families' hands instead of paying down debt faster. The model gives no credit for better health or a lower risk of financial crises.

Will it kill jobs?

No layoffs are built into it. The model shows people working fewer hours, about 1.6% fewer in Year 1 and 1.3% by Year 10, mostly lower-income workers who gain coverage, child care, paid leave and a tax cut and choose to work a bit less. The model treats the tax cut as extra income and gives no credit for lower tax rates making work pay more, so this is likely overstated. It is a real effect and we report it, but it is not the same as jobs destroyed.

Is it a tax increase on the middle class?

No. It is a tax cut: the 12% income-tax bracket drops to 10% (Sec. 2421), and there is no income-tax increase for anyone earning under $400,000. Two parts do reach middle-income households, and we say so: the carbon fee raises some energy prices (households below 400% of the poverty line get bill credits, in full up to 300%), and paid leave is funded by a 0.4% contribution from each worker, matched by employers, in exchange for 12 weeks of paid leave.

Do middle-class families come out ahead?

Yes. In our model, households up to about the 90th percentile of lifetime income consume more in Year 1, and households up to the 70th percentile still consume more in Year 10. Households from the 70th to the 90th percentile are about even by Year 10. Gains are largest for the bottom half. The top 1% consume about 2% to 11% less.

Does your economic model count everything the bill does for families?

Not everything. It now counts the middle-class tax cut, lower drug prices in private plans, the hospital price limit, the overdraft and loan-rate caps, and the productivity of roads and research. It still gives no value to removing medical debt from credit reports, to counting rent and utility payments toward credit, to protection against a financial crisis, or to health coverage beyond its cost. Those benefits land heavily on working and middle-class families, so the real picture for them is better than the model shows.

Is the $3.2 trillion in deficit reduction real?

It is our model's estimate, built line by line from CBO, JCT and Treasury figures and published in Schedule G. Even with it, the deficit stays above the bill's own limits, so the bill requires Congress to close the rest or have it closed automatically. CBO has not scored the bill, and its score will control.

Is this a government takeover of health care?

No. The Basic Minimum Plan is delivered by private insurers through the existing marketplaces and pays doctors and hospitals at Medicare rates. It is open to the roughly 30 million uninsured; we expect about 25.5 million to enroll.

Does it cut Social Security?

No. The bill makes no change to Social Security benefits, the retirement age or cost-of-living raises. It adds revenue to the trust funds from wages above $400,000 (Sec. 2408), and automatic budget cuts can never touch Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, veterans or SNAP.

Does it cut defense?

It caps total discretionary spending, defense and non-defense alike, to grow at inflation minus 1.5% a year. By the tenth year that is about 14% less, after inflation, than current law would spend. Veterans' medical care is protected.

Will companies move investment overseas?

The corporate rate stays at 21%, and the U.S. adopts the 15% global minimum tax that 140 countries agreed to, which makes moving profits offshore much harder. In our model business investment is lower than it would otherwise be through Year 20, mainly because the bill puts more money in families' hands instead of paying down debt faster. We publish that result.

Why should anyone trust your numbers?

Because you do not have to. Every revenue line has its source in Schedule G, the tax provisions were scored with the open-source Tax-Calculator model, and the economic model, its inputs and its results are published so anyone can rerun them. We publish the results that cut against us too, like the ones on this page.
