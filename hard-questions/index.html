Hard questions, answered straight

Every big bill has costs as well as benefits. These are the questions critics ask about the NSRA, with the answers our own numbers give, including the ones that cut against us. All of it can be checked: the model inputs and results are published, and CBO has not yet scored the bill.

What our own economic model shows

We ran the open-source OG-USA model of the U.S. economy on the bill as written. We ran it ourselves, so it is our analysis, not an independent score.

Year 1Year 10Year 20
Size of the economy (GDP)−1.5%−1.2%−0.6%
Federal debt, share of GDP1.6 points higher10 points lower26 points lower
Business investment−3.3%−1.2%+1.0%
Hours worked−0.9%−0.4%−0.2%

The trade is plain: a somewhat smaller economy for the first two decades in exchange for far less federal debt, coverage for about 25 million people, and lower costs for most lower-income families. We think that trade is worth making. You can judge for yourself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will the NSRA shrink the economy?

In our model, yes, modestly at first: GDP is about 1.5% lower in Year 1, 1.2% lower in Year 10 and 0.6% lower in Year 20, while federal debt falls by about 26 points of GDP by Year 20 and business investment ends up higher. The model gives no credit for roads, research or better health, so this is a cautious estimate. A smaller deficit always reads as lower spending today in this kind of model.

Will it kill jobs?

No layoffs are built into it. The model shows people working slightly fewer hours, about 0.9% fewer in Year 1 and 0.4% by Year 10, mostly lower-income workers who gain coverage, child care and paid leave and choose to work a bit less. That is a real effect and we report it, but it is not the same as jobs destroyed.

Is it a tax increase on the middle class?

There is no income-tax increase for anyone earning under $400,000. Three parts do reach middle-income households, and we say so: the carbon fee raises some energy prices (households below 300% of the poverty line get bill credits, in full up to 200%), the fee on the U.S. gross receipts of companies with more than $100 million in receipts can pass into prices, and paid leave is funded by a 0.4% contribution from each worker, matched by employers, in exchange for 12 weeks of paid leave.

Do middle-class families come out ahead?

Lower-income families do. In our model, households in the middle of the income range consume about 1% less in the first decades, because the bill uses most of its new revenue to cut the deficit rather than give it back. When we tested returning those savings to every adult instead, about 70% of households came out ahead. That is the real choice in the bill: paying down debt now versus more money in families' pockets now.

Is the $5.5 trillion in deficit reduction real?

It is our model's estimate, built line by line from CBO, JCT and Treasury figures and published in Schedule G. Even with it, the deficit stays above the bill's own limits, so the bill requires Congress to close the rest or have it closed automatically. CBO has not scored the bill, and its score will control.

Is this a government takeover of health care?

No. The Basic Minimum Plan is delivered by private insurers through the existing marketplaces and pays doctors and hospitals at Medicare rates. It is open to the roughly 30 million uninsured; we expect about 25.5 million to enroll.

Does it cut Social Security?

Not for anyone retired or eligible before 2032. For future retirees, benefits grow more slowly for the highest earners and the minimum benefit gets stronger. Automatic budget cuts can never touch Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, veterans or SNAP.

Does it cut defense?

It caps total discretionary spending, defense and non-defense alike, to grow at inflation minus 1.5% a year. By the tenth year that is about 14% less, after inflation, than current law would spend. Veterans' medical care is protected.

Will companies move investment overseas?

The corporate rate goes to 25%, still well below the 35% rate before 2017, and the U.S. adopts the 15% global minimum tax that 140 countries agreed to, which makes moving profits offshore much harder. In our model investment dips in the first years and is above where it would otherwise be by Year 20.

Why should anyone trust your numbers?

Because you do not have to. Every revenue line has its source in Schedule G, the tax provisions were scored with the open-source Tax-Calculator model, and the economic model, its inputs and its results are published so anyone can rerun them. We publish the results that cut against us too, like the ones on this page.
