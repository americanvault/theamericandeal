The American Deal · Employers and investors

What the NSRA means for employers and investors

The NSRA raises the corporate rate from 21% to 28%, closes narrow corporate loopholes, raises taxes on income above $400,000 and cuts them for the middle class, and it says so in the text. It also cuts the costs business complains about most: health care, slow federal approvals, fragile supply chains, a growing federal debt and the risk of another 2008. This page sets out both columns with the section numbers, so you can do the math for your own company.

Download the two-page brief (PDF)

What business gets

Lower drug prices in every plan you pay for

Drugs built on federally funded research cannot be priced in the U.S. above the median G7 price, and that ceiling applies to every buyer, private plans included (Sec. 1301). Insulin is capped at $35 a month and essential medicines at $100 a month out of pocket, whatever the insurance (Sec. 1303). Employer family coverage averaged $28,303 in 2026, and workers paid $7,471 of it, so employers carried about $20,800 per family plan. When Milliman modeled a comparable policy for West Health in 2021, extending negotiated drug prices to private insurance under H.R. 3, it estimated employers would pay $195 billion less in premiums from 2023 to 2029. That is an estimate for a different bill, not for the NSRA; no one has yet estimated the NSRA's effect on employer premiums.

Source: Bill Secs. 1301, 1303; KFF Employer Health Benefits Survey, Oct. 22, 2026; West Health / Milliman, May 20, 2021

Coverage for workers, with no employer mandate

The Basic Minimum Plan is open to working-age adults without affordable employer coverage, and to every uninsured child, through private insurers on the existing exchanges (Secs. 2102, 2104). The bill adds no employer mandate. It leaves employer plans and their tax exclusion untouched, and employers may offer cheaper plans that wrap around the BMP for workers enrolled in it (Sec. 2105(e)).

Source: Bill Secs. 2102, 2104(d), 2105(e)

Federal approvals on a fixed clock

For infrastructure projects of $50 million or more financed through the bill's investment fund, deadline extensions for federal review are capped at one year unless the sponsor agrees, every federal approval goes on one public schedule, and lawsuits must be filed within 150 days (Sec. 1707). Hydropower licensing and relicensing move to a fixed schedule (Sec. 1704). Environmental, safety and labor standards are unchanged.

Source: Bill Secs. 1704, 1707

Investment the private sector can join

Public infrastructure capital requires private or state co-investment of at least $1 for every federal $1 in manufacturing and energy, with a target of $3 private for every $1 public (Sec. 007). Semiconductor and critical-materials investment runs at $15 billion a year (Sec. 1204). At least 80% of the active ingredients in essential generic drugs must be made in the U.S. within five years, with $2 billion to build the plants (Sec. 1302). The government may not take ownership stakes in private companies except to recover fraud, and must sell any such stake to a domestic private buyer (Sec. 2001(a)).

Source: Bill Secs. 007, 1204, 1302, 2001(a)

A level field against offshore profit shifting

Foreign earnings of U.S. companies are taxed at the full U.S. rate, country by country, so a dollar booked in a tax haven is taxed like a dollar earned at home (Sec. 2411). Companies that pay their taxes here stop competing against ones that book profits in tax havens. The carbon fee carries a border adjustment so domestic producers are not undercut by imports from countries without one (Sec. 2410).

Source: Bill Secs. 2410, 2411

Protection against another 2008

From the third quarter of 2007 to the first quarter of 2009, U.S. household net worth fell $16 trillion, 24%. The Dallas Fed put the cost of the crisis at $6 trillion to $14 trillion of lost output, $50,000 to $120,000 for every household. The bill sets a mortgage underwriting floor, makes lenders keep at least 5% of the loans they securitize, holds the largest banks' leverage ratios at their January 1, 2025 levels, brings private credit into the stress tests and reporting, and limits credit-rating conflicts (Secs. 1605 to 1611). Those rules protect asset values, and most of the wealth lost in a crisis is held by the people with the most of it.

Source: Bill Secs. 1605-1611; Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, Staff Paper No. 20, July 2013

Customers with more to spend

The 12% income-tax bracket drops to 10%, about $85 billion a year, recaptured from incomes above $400,000 (Sec. 2421). In our run of the open-source OG-USA model, households up to about the 90th percentile of lifetime income spend more in Year 1, and households up to the 70th percentile still spend more in Year 10. Retailers, homebuilders and consumer brands sell to those households.

Source: Bill Sec. 2421; NSRA run of OG-USA 0.6.2, published at /data/open-scoring/og-usa/

Less federal debt

In the same model run, federal debt is about 4 points of GDP lower by Year 10 and 13 points lower by Year 20.

Source: NSRA run of OG-USA 0.6.2, published at /data/open-scoring/og-usa/

Small and mid-size business

  • Businesses with under $10 million in revenue are exempt from every Covered Entity obligation in the bill (Sec. 003(3)).
  • The corporate rate and base changes apply only to C corporations. S corporations, partnerships and sole proprietorships are untouched by them, and the inventory and advertising changes do not apply to firms under the small-business gross-receipts test (Sec. 2409(g)).
  • The excess-profits assessment exempts banks under $10 billion in assets (Sec. 2415).
  • Small businesses get 60 days of free compliance help before any penalty, reduced penalties under $10 million in revenue, and an appeal to the SBA's Office of Advocacy (Sec. 2001(c)).
  • Firms under $25 million in revenue, worker cooperatives and community lenders can apply for Main Street capital grants (Secs. 003(10), 204).

Rules you can plan around

Every rule here is written into statute with an effective date, a phase-in and a right to notice and appeal before anything is taken (Title XVIII). Required sales are at appraised market value with Court of Federal Claims review. Rules written that way are easier to plan around than rules that change with each administration or each enforcement priority.

The cost, in our own numbers

Our own model shows what this costs. GDP is about 2.2% lower in Year 1, 1.8% lower in Year 10 and 1.2% lower in Year 20, and business investment is lower than it would otherwise be through Year 20 (5.2% lower in Year 1, 0.8% lower in Year 20), mainly because the bill puts more money in families' hands instead of paying down debt faster. In Year 10, households in the top 1% consume about 2% to 11% less than they otherwise would. The model counts lower drug prices and the productivity of roads and research, but not a lower risk of a financial crisis. CBO has not scored the bill. See Hard questions for the full results.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the NSRA raise taxes on business?

Yes, on larger ones. The corporate rate rises from 21% to 28%, several narrow preferences end, buybacks are taxed more, and multinationals pay the full U.S. rate on foreign earnings. Small businesses and pass-throughs are largely outside these changes. The full list, with section numbers, is on this page.

Why would a business support a bill that raises its taxes?

Because the bill goes after costs that weigh on most companies: health premiums, slow federal approvals, supply-chain risk and the damage a financial crisis does. For large, highly profitable companies the higher rate may cost more than those savings. Whether the trade is good for a given company depends on its tax position, its workforce and its industry. This page gives the numbers to work it out.

Will my company's health costs fall?

Drug prices in private plans fall for drugs built on federally funded research, and insulin and essential medicines are capped at the pharmacy. How much of that reaches your premiums depends on your plan and your insurer. No one has estimated the NSRA's effect on employer premiums yet. The closest published estimate, for a different bill, found $195 billion in employer premium savings over seven years.

Does the government take over companies or industries?

No. The bill bars federal ownership stakes in private companies except to recover fraud, and any such stake must be sold to a domestic private buyer (Sec. 2001(a)). Required divestitures are at appraised market value with Court of Federal Claims review.

What do the bank rules do?

They hold the largest banks' leverage ratios at the levels in effect on January 1, 2025, add losses on loans to private-credit lenders to the stress tests, require lenders to keep at least 5% of loans they securitize, and set a mortgage underwriting floor. Community banks are not touched by the leverage floor and are exempt from the excess-profits assessment.

Is the economy smaller under the bill?

In our model, modestly, and we publish it: GDP about 1.8% lower in Year 10 and 1.2% lower in Year 20, with federal debt 22 points of GDP lower by Year 20 and business investment still somewhat lower. An earlier version without the middle-class tax cut grew back faster; we chose to put more money in families' hands. CBO has not scored the bill.
